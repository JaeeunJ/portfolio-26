03Freelance · Pitch competition

Designing a healthcare app’s first happy path in one week

Project Vitality puts scheduling, records, and AI visit notes in one place — for patients and for the physicians treating them. I came on a week before a pitch competition as the only designer, and left with the branding and a working prototype of patient sign-up.

01 — The problem

Getting the healthcare you deserve shouldn’t be this hard to figure out.

Where you live decides how easily you see a doctor. People in rural areas travel hours for an appointment a city patient books in an afternoon — and even in the city, working out which kind of help you need is confusing enough that people put it off.

Care delayed that way shows up later as worse health outcomes. The client’s bet was that one system serving patients and physicians at the same time could close some of that gap.

02 — The week

Seven days, one designer, one path through the app.

This was a last-minute build for a pitch competition, so scope was the first design decision. Rather than sketch the whole product thinly, I picked one thing to finish properly: a patient signing up and booking for the first time. Everything else got a screen only if the pitch needed it.

Each stage got a hard time limit, and I moved on whether or not it felt done.

03 — Research

What the client asked for, and what the market had already learned.

The brief came in as a short list of jobs on both sides of the appointment:

  • Physicians want every appointment, virtual and in-person, in one calendar.
  • Physicians want AI to sit in on virtual visits and hand back the notes.
  • Patients want to book virtually, and to filter doctors by location.
  • Everyone wants navigation a non-technical user can get through.

Two products that got partway there

Nobody had shipped this exact thing — healthcare online and AI in the room are both new enough that the field is thin. So I looked at two apps that had done the online half.

  • Practo India

    Works Search by symptom, not by specialty, so you can find a specialist without guessing your own diagnosis first. Expert-answered forums do the same job.

    Breaks Too many type sizes and colours at once. Hard to scan if you’re older or in a hurry.

  • Rocket Doctor Canada (ON, BC, AB)

    Works A short, direct route to a confirmed appointment, connected straight to your insurance and upfront about cost.

    Breaks Web only, and rough on a phone. You pick a format — text, call, video, in-person — rather than a specialist.

What that changed

  • Split the surfaces: a fuller mobile app for younger patients, a simpler website for older ones.
  • Put the virtual-or-in-person choice at the very top of the home screen.
  • Give common symptoms their own shelf on home, so nobody has to name their condition.
  • Less text, fewer bright colours.

04 — Who it’s for

Two patients at opposite ends of the same gap.

The competitor work and a second round with the client pointed at the same split: someone older and further out, and someone younger in the city who doesn’t know the system.

  • Da Kim, 61 Welland, ON · retired teacher
    About

    Da values her independence and needs semi-regular checkups, but mobility and transport make the trip to her specialist hard. COVID got her comfortable with virtual visits, and she’d rather keep them.

    Pain points
    • Forgets when checkups are due
    • Travel is physically tiring
    • Little access to care without going in person
  • William Li, 20 Toronto, ON · university student
    About

    William moved from China for university and doesn’t know how Canadian healthcare works. His allergies get severe seasonally, so he needs specialists and a place to keep track of them.

    Pain points
    • Doesn’t know the system
    • Needs to be pointed at the right specialist, not search for one

05 — Visual design

Clean, with enough personality to not feel like a clinic.

Branding came before the screens, because other deadlines needed it first. The client wanted clean with a bit of play. Green was the obvious base — it’s what people already read as life, which is the name — paired with a sans-serif that isn’t cold.

06 — Final designs

A system first, then the screens that came out of it.

Primitives went in before components — with four days left, the only way to draw quickly was to not re-decide colour and type on every screen. The component library is still partial; the project is paused until my term ends.

07 — Where it landed

We’re still going.

We didn’t take the competition, but the pitch pulled in several interested parties, and I’m picking the app back up after this school term.

What I’d do next

  1. User tests

    Skipped under the time crunch. Moderated and unmoderated, as many as I can get, before anything else ships.

  2. The website

    The app was the MVP. Competitive analysis made it clear the older half of the audience needs the web version.

  3. The physical product

    Early days, but the real goal is de-centralizing hospitals — a device that replaces the in-person consultation.

  4. Beyond Canada

    A long way off, but it changes what we build now if we mean it.

What I took away

  1. Order is the design

    When navigation has to be simple enough for anyone, information architecture does most of the work. What came first mattered more than what it looked like.

  2. Feedback has a cost

    On a week-long schedule, knowing when to stop and ask — and how much to fold in — was the hardest call I made repeatedly.

  3. Devs were building while I designed

    No handoff moment, just a running conversation I wasn’t good enough at yet.

  4. Client goals aren’t user goals

    They mostly overlapped here. User testing is where I’ll find out where they don’t.
