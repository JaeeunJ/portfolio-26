05SYDE 162 · Coursework

Three days to make a campus you can actually navigate

UW Navi was the final project for SYDE 162, a human–computer interaction course. Six of us had a long weekend to answer one question: why is finding a room at Waterloo so much harder than finding the building it’s in?

Role
UI/UX design, prototyping, user testing, branding
Timeline
August 1–3, 2022 · 3 days
Team
Me, sole designer · 2 researchers, 3 writers
Tools
Figma, FigJam
Prototype
Open in Figma→

01 — The problem

Outdoor maps stop at the front door.

Getting to a classroom at Waterloo means switching tools halfway. Google Maps gets you to a building and then quits — and it drops its pin in the middle of the building, which in a large one isn’t near any entrance, let alone the entrance closest to your room.

Inside, you’re on static floor plans bolted to walls at intervals nobody chose for your benefit. They’re unintuitive enough that students get lost and take routes that cost them time they didn’t have.

Everything else about moving around campus has the same shape:

  • Campus food vendors mostly have no reviews, and their own descriptions are written to sell.
  • Study spaces vary in ways no map records — half the desk lamps in one Davis Centre area don’t work, and you only find out by sitting down.
  • Parts of campus aren’t wheelchair-accessible, and no widely used map app will filter a route by accessibility or by weather.

02 — The solution

One map that goes from the sidewalk to the seat.

A navigation system for everyone on campus, where the outdoor map and the building’s floor plan are the same continuous thing — so a route ends at a room, not at a postcode. Around that, the parts that make the route worth trusting: filters for the fastest, indoor, or wheelchair-accessible way; ratings on study spots, classrooms, and eateries; and your friends, if you want them to see you.

03 — Research

Everyone we asked had already been lost.

What we set out to build

  • Instant navigation to a place — or to a friend
  • A friending system, where showing your location is a choice
  • Ratings on study spots, classrooms, and eateries
  • Onboarding with authentication, since location is the data at stake
  • Search, and route filters for fastest, indoors, or wheelchair-accessible
  • A campus map that continues into the building’s floor plan

The survey

We surveyed 32 students about getting around campus. 84.4% had had trouble finding a university facility at least once — and most said they need to go somewhere three or more times before the route sticks. That second number is the one that mattered: the problem isn’t a first visit being hard, it’s that it stays hard.

Moderated tests

Preliminary questions first, then prompts to work through, which gave us the route people actually take rather than the one they describe. It confirmed the pain points we’d guessed at — and turned up one we hadn’t: a participant pointed out that Google Maps’ default path across campus goes up a flight of stairs, with no wheelchair-accessible alternative offered at all.

The decision matrix

The friend features were the ones we argued about, so we scored them against the two things the app is for: does this help people socialize, and does it help them navigate?

Summoning a friend and locating a friend scored top marks on both, so they were in. The status update feature only served the social half — it does nothing to make navigation easier. We kept it anyway, deliberately: it’s what brings people back into the app, and an app nobody opens can’t route anyone anywhere.

04 — Design system

Bright teal, because being lost is annoying enough.

A cheerful teal as the base colour — enough to stand out from the grey-and-blue field every other map app lives in, and enough to make an errand nobody enjoys a bit lighter.

One sans-serif throughout, matching how casual the app is meant to feel. Nothing here needs a second voice.

We were short on time and I built the component set anyway — the most-used components and the iconography. On a three-day build, drawing a button twice costs more than defining it once.

05 — Where it landed

The first thing I designed on my own.

This was the first time I designed a whole application without other designers on it, and it was a great deal more work than it looks from the outside. It was also due the week before finals, which I’d call the real constraint. Given that, I’m happy with where it got to.

What I’d do next

  1. More components

    The set covers what we drew, not what the app would need. A new user would feel the seams in the places we ran out of time to systematize.

  2. Test the accessible routes

    The strongest finding came from one participant and one flight of stairs. That deserved its own round of testing, not a feature.

What I took away

  1. A feature can earn its place two ways

    The status update does nothing for navigation and we kept it, because getting people to open the app is upstream of everything the app does.

  2. The useful number wasn’t the big one

    84% having trouble says there’s a problem. Three visits before a route sticks says what the problem is.

  3. A system is worth it even at three days

    Especially at three days. It was the only reason six people’s work looked like one app.
