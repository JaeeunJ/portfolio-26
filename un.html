05SYDE 162 · Coursework

Three days to make a campus you can actually navigate

UW Navi was the final project for my SYDE 162 human-computer interaction course. Six of us had a long weekend to answer one core question: why is finding a specific room on the Waterloo campus so much harder than finding the building itself?

Role
UI/UX design, prototyping, user testing, branding
Timeline
August 1-3, 2022 · 3 days
Team
Me, sole designer · 2 researchers, 3 writers
Tools
Figma , FigJam

01 — The problem

Existing outdoor maps fail at the front door.

Navigating to a classroom at Waterloo forces students to switch tools halfway. Google Maps gets you to a building and drops a pin in the dead center. In a massive facility, that pin is nowhere near the entrance closest to your room.

Once inside, you rely on static floor plans mounted on walls at random intervals. They are so unintuitive that students regularly get lost and take inefficient routes that cost them time they do not have.

This friction exists across the entire campus experience:

  • Campus food vendors lack student reviews, relying only on promotional descriptions.
  • Study spaces vary in unrecorded ways. You often only discover a broken desk lamp or missing outlet after sitting down.
  • Significant parts of campus are not wheelchair-accessible, and no standard map app filters routes by accessibility or weather.

02 — The solution

A continuous map from the sidewalk to the seat.

I designed a unified navigation system where the outdoor map and the indoor floor plan connect seamlessly, ending routes at a specific room rather than a postcode. I surrounded that core map with trusted context: route filters for speed, indoor paths, and accessibility. I also included peer ratings for study spots and eateries alongside an opt-in location sharing system to find friends on campus.

03 — Research

Surveys revealed that poor navigation forces students to memorize complex routes.

Establishing the core feature set

  • Instant navigation to a specific room or friend
  • An opt-in friending and location-sharing system
  • Peer ratings for study spots, classrooms, and eateries
  • Onboarding with authentication to protect location data
  • Route filters for fastest, indoors, or wheelchair-accessible paths
  • A seamless outdoor-to-indoor mapping transition

Validating the problem through student surveys

We surveyed 32 students about campus navigation. 84.4% reported trouble finding a university facility. Crucially, most said they needed to visit a location three or more times before memorizing the route. That metric defined the real problem: navigating is not just hard the first time, it stays hard.

Refining requirements through moderated testing

We ran participants through preliminary questions and prompt-based tasks to observe actual navigation behavior rather than self-reported habits. This confirmed our suspected pain points and uncovered a major accessibility flaw. One participant noted that Google Maps routes students up a central campus staircase without offering a wheelchair-accessible alternative.

Prioritizing features with a decision matrix

Because the social features caused team debate, we scored them against two core product goals: facilitating socialization and improving navigation.

Summoning and locating friends scored high on both metrics. The status update feature only served socialization and offered no navigation value. We chose to keep it anyway because social engagement drives daily active use, and an app nobody opens cannot route anyone anywhere.

04 — Design system

A bright teal palette offsets the frustration of getting lost.

I selected a cheerful teal as the base color to stand out from the standard grey and blue used in existing map apps. The brighter tone helps lighten the frustration of a stressful campus commute.

I used a single sans-serif typeface throughout the interface to match the casual, peer-to-peer feel of the app.

Despite the tight three-day timeline, I built a foundational component set covering primary interactions and iconography. Defining a button once is always faster than drawing it repeatedly during a sprint.

05 — Where it landed

Shipping a cohesive application design in three days.

This was my first time designing a complete application independently without senior design support, completing it just days before final exams. Given the severe time constraints, I am proud of the cohesive final prototype.

Future priorities: expanding the system and testing accessibility

  1. Expand the component library

    The current system only covers the screens we had time to draw. A new user would notice inconsistencies in edge cases we lacked time to systematize.

  2. Test accessible routes extensively

    Our strongest accessibility insight came from a single user testing session. That finding warranted a dedicated round of testing rather than a quick feature add.

Key takeaways: earning screen space, interpreting data, and systematizing early.

  1. Features earn their place in different ways

    We kept the social status update despite it lacking navigation utility because user engagement is a prerequisite for functional utility.

  2. The most useful metric is rarely the biggest one

    The 84% failure rate proved the problem existed, but the statistic showing users needed three visits to learn a route defined exactly what the problem was.

  3. Design systems save time on any timeline

    Building components during a three-day sprint was the only reason six different contributors produced a unified final product.
