Designing a livestream censor, then designing it again

LiveGuard monitors a livestream in real time and mutes sensitive audio before it causes demonetization. As the sole designer collaborating with five engineers, I initially designed the comprehensive feature set requested by the client, then iteratively simplified the product into an MVP that fit our development timeline.

Role
UI/UX design, prototyping, branding
Timeline
August 2023 → ongoing
Team
Me, solo designer · 5 devs
Tools
Figma , FigJam

01 — The problem

Demonetization risks force streamers to spend hours manually editing.

Platforms frequently revoke monetization when content breaches community or advertiser guidelines. For creators streaming for multiple hours at a time, a single unscripted phrase can jeopardize the entire broadcast.

Most creators currently review hours of recorded streams after the broadcast just to cut out violations. LiveGuard aims to solve this upfront by catching and muting violations live as they happen.

02 — The solution

A companion web app monitors the stream without disrupting existing creator tools.

Streamers rely on established software like OBS and prefer not to introduce complex routing right before going live. I designed LiveGuard as a lightweight companion app that runs alongside their existing broadcast rather than acting as a gatekeeper.

To align the team early, I standardized three core system definitions:

  1. Action
    1. The specific audio or video trigger flagged by the user.
  2. Event
    1. The trigger combined with its automated response (what is flagged and how it gets muted).
  3. Streaming profile
    1. A bundled group of events designed to activate together.

03 — First pass

The initial concept translated every client requirement into an expansive feature suite.

Because there were no direct competitor tools in the live censorship space, I collaborated directly with team members who actively stream to establish the initial product requirements.

Balancing complex client requests with essential user controls

The client initially proposed deep customization pages, separate trigger and event libraries, analytics dashboards, and an in-stream overlay.

Through user research, I identified that creators primarily needed high-contrast status feedback, clear connection indicators, and simple controls that could be adjusted quickly during a broadcast.

04 — The cut

Engineering constraints and tight timelines required cutting non-essential features.

As we evaluated our delivery milestones, our five developers needed to concentrate primarily on the core machine learning models. I reassessed the scope to reduce complexity for both the engineers and first-time users.

  1. Fixed delivery schedule

    The planned launch dates left no room for secondary dashboards, requiring us to prioritize core functionality.

  2. Engineering bandwidth

    Because the engineering team focused heavily on audio detection accuracy, I minimized interface overhead.

  3. Onboarding simplicity

    Without familiar mental models in the market, reducing early interface density made the product much easier to learn.

05 — Second pass

The redesigned interface condensed the workflow into a single, high-contrast control panel.

I stripped away the multi-page navigation and nested libraries, consolidating the experience into a direct monitoring dashboard. I structured the layout so creators can verify active triggers and view muted audio events with a glance at a secondary monitor.

Consolidating event management and live monitoring into one screen

Instead of separating triggers, actions, and profiles into individual views, I grouped them into modular toggle cards. Creators can enable preset filters or adjust sensitivity settings quickly without losing sight of their live stream status.

06 — User testing

Testing with active streamers verified that low visual competition is critical during broadcasts.

I tested the simplified prototype with content creators during live and simulated streaming sessions. Their feedback reinforced that secondary screens must not create visual distraction while games or chat windows are open.

Refining live feedback states and status indicators

Users emphasized the importance of knowing exactly when an audio cut occurs. In response, I designed subtle, high-visibility visual pulses that confirm active muting without drawing excessive focus away from the broadcast.

And then, one more cut

Late on, technical constraints took two more things off the board- analytics and the in-stream overlay were completely removed.

07 — Design system

A high-contrast, tokenized design system.

I established a dark, monochrome theme designed specifically for multi-monitor streaming setups. The restrained palette keeps interface elements legible in low-light desk environments while keeping the focus on status indicators.

Building reusable components for rapid iteration

I created a structured Figma component library with clear token naming for colors, typography, and interactive states. This enabled the development team to implement changes quickly without needing custom specs for each screen.

08 — Where it landed

Iterating directly against technical constraints resulted in a focused, shippable MVP.

This was my first freelance project taken end to end. It was also my first time navigating limited engineering resources and evolving requirements during a project.

Future priorities: broader usability testing, stream edge cases, and a complete component library.

  1. Run more user tests earlier

    The feedback from the first three testing sessions provided the clearest insights of the project. I would schedule user testing earlier in the sprint cycle to validate technical assumptions sooner.

  2. Design for broadcast edge cases

    The current flows focus on ideal stream conditions. I want to account for live failures, including sudden audio desyncs, platform API drops, and rapid-fire false positives.

  3. Complete the full design system

    With five developers building simultaneously, an incomplete component library creates implementation gaps. Finishing every variant and interaction token will reduce front-end guesswork.

Key takeaways: scoping early, designing for low attention, and collaborating closely with engineering.

  1. Aligning design with development constraints early

    Recognizing engineering priorities allowed me to simplify the UI quickly rather than defending features that could not be built in time.

  2. Designing for secondary attention

    Because streamers focus primarily on their audience and game, the companion UI had to communicate status with minimal visual clutter.

  3. Establishing shared vocabulary upfront

    Defining technical terms like Actions and Events early reduced miscommunication between design, engineering, and the client.
