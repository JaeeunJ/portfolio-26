02Freelance · 2-month project

Making calorie tracking feel like texting

Callie is an iOS calorie tracker built around conversation. Users can send a photo or describe a meal to get a full macro breakdown, then make quick adjustments like "half the bread" or "no cheese." As the sole designer, I led the project from initial user research to developer handoff over 1.5 months, working in close sprints with the client.

Role
UX research, UI/UX design, branding
Timeline
May 2026 · 10 days
Team
Me, sole designer · client (dev)
Tools

01 — The problem

Existing trackers fail because manual logging takes more effort than eating.

The client found that many consistent calorie trackers eventually quit because current apps require too much effort. Searching a database to log ingredients one by one often takes longer than cooking the meal itself. Meanwhile, photo-based tools output rigid calorie numbers that leave little room for error correction.

He identified the core issue as a lack of conversational control. When users cannot say "actually it was half a cup" without starting over or navigating complex menus, the friction leads them to abandon the tool.

02 — The bet

Replacing databases with conversation cuts logging down to 30 secs.

My focus was designing a direct logging loop. In Callie, users open the app directly to a chat screen, input a meal using natural text or a photo, and receive an instant breakdown of calories, protein, carbs, and fat ready to confirm in one tap.

I structured the experience around three core product principles:

  • Conversational refinement: Simple follow-ups like "no rice, extra guac" update the existing entry without starting over.
  • Agent memory: Callie learns recurring routines so you can simply log "my usual morning shake."
  • Transparency and trust: The chat clearly shows how the description converted into macros, with quick manual overrides available at any point.

03 — Research

Competitive teardowns showed that lengthy onboarding and guilt-tripping notifications drive churn.

The client's PRD established clear core requirements while highlighting key unknowns: onboarding depth, notification strategy, and memory granularity. My research focused on answering those critical questions.

Onboarding: open directly into chat and defer biometrics.

I combed through reviews of MyFitnessPal, MacroFactor, and CalAI, and noted users often complain about the 20 to 50 screens of setup between the app opening and their first log. To reinforce Callie's speed, I designed the onboarding to drop users straight into the chat within three screens, deferring profile setup until after their first meal is recorded.

Replace nagging alerts with opt-in, routine-based nudges.

My research into user sentiment showed that generic notification reminders often make users feel guilty, which frequently causes them to uninstall the app. I replaced standard reminders with optional, habit-based nudges tied directly to user routines, like logging a shake after a workout.

Track meals by natural nicknames, not rigid ingredient lists.

I observed that people naturally refer to frequent meals by nicknames rather than complete ingredient lists. Because camera estimates can miss hidden ingredients like cooking oil, I designed the system to recognize meal nicknames while keeping individual ingredients easily editable underneath.

04 — Branding

High-energy red and expressive typography give functional numbers personality.

I built the typography system by pairing SF Pro for standard interface components with Bricolage Grotesque for macro numbers and Spline Sans for readable ingredient lists.

I selected an energetic red-orange (#FF3C00) as the primary brand color, based on the client's preferences, applying it to primary actions and as a subtle 10% wash for backgrounds. I organized these into design tokens early so they would be simple to implement.

05 — The chat

The home screen turns conversations and quick photos into macro breakdowns.

I kept the chat interface minimal by placing daily targets in the header and leaving the central canvas open. Users can tap the screen to open the camera, choose from recent meal suggestions, or upload a photo to quickly receive and confirm their breakdown.

Receipt-style cards for single snacks to all-day multi-meal dumps.

I designed responsive cards that scale depending on what the user inputs. Single items feature quick modification tags, while multiple meals collapse into an itemized receipt format with a single button to confirm the entire group.

To keep the space tidy, I removed persistent chat history. If a user leaves mid-entry, I surfaced a clear banner on the home screen so they can resume where they left off.

06 — The log

View daily macro totals and entries together.

I explored two layout directions for the daily summary: horizontal progress bars beside the calorie ring, and a stacked vertical format. Since the client was also our target user, I shared both options during our review to select the most readable view together.

Past entries stay fully editable through conversations.

In the meal detail view, I included an optional photo, macro totals, timestamps, and a collapsed ingredient list. Users can tap any field to edit details directly, or tap "Edit with AI," which is pinned to the bottom of the screen.

Edit with AI reopens the conversation about that meal (“bro u forgot the carrots”) and the log updates the same way it was created. Correction is the product’s signature move, so it works on old entries exactly like new ones.

07 — Settings

Account setup and smart notifications remain strictly opt-in.

I placed non-essential controls in Settings to keep the main chat uncluttered. This includes optional Apple or Google authentication, Apple Health sync options, and profile preferences.

I made notifications a clear, single opt-in toggle so users can easily manage automated reminders on their own terms.

08 — Where it landed

Shipped a production-ready iOS design system and core flows.

Over 1.5 months of sprints and weekly syncs with the client, I created the end-to-end design and organized complete specs and components in Figma for development.

Key takeaways: tight client alignment, validating PRDs, and introducing novelty sparingly.

  1. The client was the primary user

    Collaborating with a stakeholder who represented our target persona helped me validate layout options quickly and resolve questions with fast feedback loops.

  2. Using research to resolve open questions rather than redoing work

    Because the initial product requirements were already grounded, I focused my research sprints on resolving specific edge cases and reducing drop-off points.

  3. Relying on familiar interaction patterns where appropriate

    I used conventional patterns for the log and dashboard views so users would already understand how to read their daily progress, focusing their attention on the new chat-based logging flow.

  4. Iterating in weekly check-ins

    Regular check-ins prevented unnecessary redesigns and helped us make practical decisions about defaults and interface density early in the process.
