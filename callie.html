02Freelance · 2-month project

Making calorie tracking feel like texting

Callie is an iOS calorie tracker disguised as a chat: send a photo or describe your meal, get a full macro breakdown, and refine it like a conversation — “half the bread,” “no cheese.” I was the sole designer, taking it from research to handoff in ten days of daily check-ins with the founder.

01 — The problem

Tracking a meal takes more effort than choosing it.

People who already believe in calorie tracking still quit doing it, because every tool makes them do the thinking. On one side is the database grind — searching, scrolling, and assembling a five-ingredient dinner one ingredient at a time, which takes longer than the cooking did. On the other is the black-box AI: photo in, number out, take it or leave it.

The gap between them is conversational control. Nobody lets you say “actually it was half a cup” and just have the number adjust — you start over, or you dig through menus. The tracking itself becomes the friction, and the friction is why people churn.

02 — The bet

Chat is the interface, and the agent does the thinking.

Callie’s core loop: open the app and land in a fresh chat, tell it what you ate — by photo or in plain language — and an agent returns the full breakdown: calories, carbs, protein, fat. Never just a calorie number. One tap logs it. The target was a meal logged in under thirty seconds.

What makes it a product rather than a prompt wrapper is three commitments:

  • Conversational refinement — “no rice, extra guac, half the cheese” adjusts the entry like a person would, no starting over.
  • Agent memory — say “my usual morning shake” and Callie already knows, because it learns your meals and habits over time.
  • Trust through transparency — the chat itself is the proof. You can see how your words became the breakdown, and edit the numbers directly if you disagree.

03 — Research

The PRD ended in questions, so the research started there.

The founder’s PRD was thorough enough that I didn’t need to fight for features — the requirements held up. What it left open, it left open honestly, as four questions at the end of the document. My research week went at those: how onboarding should work, whether notifications belong at all, and how granular the agent’s memory needs to be.

Onboarding: don’t have one

I tore down the onboarding of MyFitnessPal, MacroFactor, and CalAI. The market leaders put 20 to 50+ screens between install and the first log — goal quizzes, biometrics, weight projections, an account wall, a paywall. For an app whose entire identity is being the easy alternative, the strongest first impression is the absence of all that: Callie opens straight into the chat, in under three screens, and asks for biometrics only after you’ve logged something.

Notifications: only if the user shapes them

Forum research turned up something sharper than annoyance: generic reminders make people feel ashamed, and shame is a reason users gave for deleting tracking apps entirely. Logging already sits in the back of their mind all day. So Callie replaces generic nagging with context-aware nudges built from the user’s own patterns — “you usually log a shake after a workout” — and makes them granular and optional, which the competitors don’t offer at all.

Memory: remember meals the way people name them

People describe repeated meals in meal units — “my usual morning shake,” not a list of ingredients — and they mostly re-log the same meals with small day-to-day tweaks. And they’re rightly skeptical of photo AI, which misses invisible calories like the 200 in the cooking oil. So the memory design stores the nickname for speed but keeps the ingredients underneath it for tweaks, and learns cooking habits — “always fries in two rounds of oil” — to close the trust gap.

04 — Branding

An orangey-red, and a typeface that isn’t allowed to be SF Pro.

The logo couldn’t just use the system font — SF Pro isn’t licensed for commercial marks — so I put up options and we chose from there. The working system is SF Pro for the interface, Bricolage Grotesque as the display face — the numbers are the loudest thing a user takes in, so they get the personality — and Spline Sans for subtext, whose tight spacing keeps long ingredient lists from blurring together.

The palette leads with #FF3C00, an orangey-red, used pure for the brand moments and at 10% opacity as its own background wash. The colour tokens went into a design system from day one so implementation wouldn’t have to reverse-engineer them.

05 — The chat

Open the app, and you’re already logging.

The chat home carries your daily progress in the header and nothing else in your way: tapping the middle of the screen opens the camera, the input field suggests meals you’ve logged before, and a + menu covers photos from the library. Describe the meal, get the breakdown, log it — with a streak moment on the way out.

When one message is four meals

Real logging is messy — one message might be a meal with sides, or an entire day. The card scales with it: a single meal with sides stays one card with modification chips; two meals duplicate the card; and at three or more, the output becomes a receipt — every meal collapsed to a line with its calorie contribution, expandable if you care, one Log button for the lot. A log-and-go user reads it as “seems about right” and moves on.

And because Callie is a go-in, go-out app, it keeps no chat history — the one thing worth preserving is a log you walked away from mid-conversation. That comes back as a “finish logging” banner on the chat home rather than a history tab.

06 — The log

The journal answers “how am I doing” at a glance.

For the daily overview I drew two layouts — macros reading left-to-right beside the calorie ring, or top-to-bottom under it — and brought both to the check-in instead of picking. The founder was the app’s primary user, which changed how I made decisions: anything that came down to reading preference was his call to make, framed with my recommendation attached.

One entry, fully editable

A log’s detail page keeps the anatomy simple: an optional photo, the meal’s name with its modification chips (“+1.5x lettuce base”), the time — auto-set from when you logged unless you said otherwise — and the ingredient list, collapsed to three until you ask for more. Every part of it can be changed after the fact: rename it, re-photograph it, move it in time, or delete it behind a native confirm.

The one button that never scrolls away is Edit with AI. It reopens the conversation about that meal — “bro u forgot the carrots” — and the log updates the same way it was created. Correction is the product’s signature move, so it works on old entries exactly like new ones.

07 — Settings

Login is optional, and so are the reminders.

Settings holds the three things the chat shouldn’t: your account (three header states — none, Apple, Google — because an account is a way to save progress, not a wall in front of the app), your connected data (Apple Health sync and the biometrics, each editable through native pickers), and your preferences.

The preference that matters is push notification nudges — the feature the research flagged as the one most likely to get the app deleted. It ships as a single honest toggle: smart reminders based on your own logging patterns, off if you say so.

08 — Where it landed

Handed off, on schedule.

Ten days, research to end-to-end package, with a check-in every day. The final screens — polished a step past what the last check-in shows — are in the Figma file, organized for handoff to development.

What I took away

  1. The client was the primary user

    That collapsed the usual distance between stakeholder and persona. Ambiguous calls became framed options with a recommendation, and the answers came back same-day.

  2. Research can validate instead of fight

    The PRD’s features survived contact with the evidence, so the research week went into its open questions — which is where it actually changed the design.

  3. Borrow familiarity, spend novelty carefully

    The journal and progress views deliberately lean on patterns users already know from CalAI. The novelty budget went to the one thing nobody else does: the conversation.

  4. Daily check-ins beat big reveals

    Nothing drifted more than a day from what the founder expected, and decisions like collapsed-versus-expanded defaults got settled while they were still cheap.
